Do You Need a Backflow Prevention Device?

By the Altadena 24/7 Plumbing team

Talk To A Plumber
(626) 898-6169
Call Now

Backflow prevention assemblies stop contaminated water from flowing backward into the public water supply, and California requires annual testing on properties that have one. The harder question for a lot of homeowners is whether they need one at all.

What Backflow Actually Means

Backflow happens when a drop in water pressure, from a water main break or heavy demand elsewhere in the system, causes water to reverse direction and flow backward through your plumbing rather than forward into it.

If your property has a connection point where contaminated water could enter the system during that reversal, an irrigation system, a fire sprinkler system, or certain commercial connections, a backflow prevention assembly is usually required at that connection point.

Who Typically Needs One

Irrigation systems are the most common residential trigger, since fertilizer and soil contaminants sitting in irrigation lines pose a real contamination risk if that water reversed into the drinking supply.

Fire sprinkler systems, increasingly required under California's updated Wildland-Urban Interface Code for rebuilt homes in fire-affected areas, also typically require backflow protection.

Certain commercial connections, particularly those with chemical processes or boiler systems, almost always require one under local cross-connection control programs.

The Legal Requirement Once You Have One

California Code of Regulations Title 17 requires annual testing of backflow prevention assemblies by a certified tester, with results filed with your local water purveyor. This is not optional once an assembly is installed. Missing the deadline typically triggers a compliance notice, and continued noncompliance can affect water service.

How To Find Out If You Have One Or Need One

If your property already has an assembly, your water purveyor will typically mail an annual reminder when testing is due. If you are not sure whether your property needs one, particularly if you are adding irrigation or planning a fire rebuild with sprinklers, a licensed plumber can confirm the requirement before installation rather than after an inspection flags it.

Why This Comes Up More In Some Altadena-Area Neighborhoods

Larger lots with irrigation systems are more common in some of the foothill communities near Altadena, like La Cañada Flintridge, than in Altadena's own more compact residential lots, which is part of why backflow testing questions come up more often from those neighboring areas.

That said, any Altadena property with irrigation, a fire sprinkler system tied to an Eaton Fire rebuild, or certain commercial features should assume the same requirement applies rather than assuming it is only relevant to larger nearby properties.

How Testing And Repair Actually Work

Testing checks the assembly's check valves and relief mechanisms under controlled conditions to confirm it will actually stop backflow if a pressure drop occurs elsewhere in the system, not just that it looks intact.

If an assembly fails testing, the specific failed component gets repaired or rebuilt, then the assembly is retested before the passing report is filed with your water purveyor, all within whatever compliance window your provider specifies.

New installations follow the same process from the start: a properly sized assembly is selected for the connection type, installed to code, and tested immediately before it is ever considered active, rather than assumed to be working simply because it was installed correctly.

Installation itself follows Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) requirements for backflow assembly placement and clearance, the same code every plumbing installation in California is held to.

Common Questions

What happens during a backflow test?

A certified tester attaches gauges to the assembly's test cocks and checks each check valve and relief valve under controlled pressure conditions, typically taking 20 to 30 minutes.

Can I install a backflow assembly myself?

Installation should be done by a licensed plumber to ensure correct assembly type and placement, since an incorrectly installed assembly can fail its very first required test.

What if I just removed my irrigation system? Do I still need testing?

If the assembly and connection point are physically removed, the requirement typically goes away, but confirm with your water purveyor rather than assuming, since some require documentation of the removal.

How much notice does my water purveyor give before a testing deadline?

Most water purveyors mail a written notice well ahead of the deadline, though the exact notice period varies by provider, which is worth confirming for your specific property.

What is the difference between an RP and a DC assembly?

A reduced pressure (RP) assembly protects against both backpressure and backsiphonage for higher-hazard connections. A double check (DC) assembly protects lower-hazard connections. Your notice specifies which type applies.

Does a backflow assembly need to be replaced eventually, or just tested?

Most assemblies last many years with proper testing and minor repairs, but internal components like rubber seals and springs do wear out over time, occasionally requiring a full assembly replacement rather than a simple repair.

The Bottom Line

Backflow prevention is one of those requirements that feels like paperwork until you understand what it is actually protecting against. If your property has irrigation, fire suppression, or certain commercial features, assume you need one and confirm rather than finding out from a compliance notice or a service interruption. A quick call to confirm your specific requirement costs far less time and hassle than discovering the issue after your water purveyor has already flagged your property as out of compliance.

What To Do Before Your Testing Deadline

  • Locate your backflow assembly, usually near the irrigation shutoff or the property's main connection point.
  • Have your water purveyor's notice available, since it specifies the exact deadline and assembly type.
  • Confirm the tester you hire is California-certified, since only a certified tester's results satisfy the Title 17 requirement.
