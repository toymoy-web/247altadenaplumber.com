How Do You Know If You Need to Repipe Your House?

By the Altadena 24/7 Plumbing team

Talk To A Plumber
(626) 898-6169
Call Now

Repiping replaces a home's interior supply lines entirely, which is a bigger project than a single repair. The real question most homeowners are trying to answer is whether they are looking at a repair or a repipe, a question that comes up often in Altadena given how much of the housing stock still runs on original pre-1960 galvanized pipe.

One Leak Is Usually A Repair

A single leak on an otherwise sound line, even an older one, is typically a spot repair rather than a sign the whole system needs replacing. Jumping straight to a full repipe after one leak is usually more than the situation calls for.

A Pattern Of Leaks Is A Different Story

More than one leak in different parts of the house within a year or two is a much stronger signal that the pipe material itself, not just one unlucky section, has reached the end of its service life.

This is especially true for pre-1960 homes still running original galvanized supply lines, which are now 65 to 100 or more years old and well past their designed lifespan in many cases.

Other Signs Beyond Leaks

Persistent rust-colored water across multiple fixtures, not just one, usually means the pipe itself is corroding internally rather than a single fixture having a problem.

A steady, gradual drop in water pressure across the whole house, without any single obvious cause, can mean corrosion has been narrowing the inside of galvanized pipe for years.

Copper Homes Can Need It Too

Mid-century homes with copper supply lines are not immune. Multiple leaks in different rooms within a short span on a copper system is still a real repipe signal, just on a different timeline than galvanized pipe typically follows.

Material Choices: Copper Or PEX

Copper has a long track record and rigid durability, at a higher material cost than PEX. PEX is flexible, faster to install in tight spaces, and performs well under the seismic movement California building codes plan for, and it is now widely code-approved throughout the state.

Why This Comes Up So Often In Altadena Specifically

Pre-1960 homes across Altadena commonly still run original galvanized supply lines that are now 65 to 100 or more years old, well past their designed service life. Repeated pinhole leaks and gradually dropping pressure are the most common signals we see on this exact housing type before a homeowner decides to repipe.

Homes going through an Eaton Fire rebuild are also a natural repipe candidate, even when parts of the original plumbing technically survived, since it is far easier to install new supply lines while walls are already open during construction than to retrofit them into a finished home later.

Planning A Repipe Around Your Home's Layout

We map a home's existing supply line layout first, identifying which walls, ceilings, or crawlspace access points the new lines will need to route through with the least disruption to finished surfaces.

Material choice between copper and PEX depends on the home's layout, budget, and seismic considerations, since PEX's flexibility performs well under the earthquake movement California building codes plan for and is now widely code-approved throughout the state.

Sequencing matters too. Working through a home section by section keeps at least some water service available throughout most of the project, rather than shutting off the entire house for the full duration.

Either material choice has to meet Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) standards and California's lead-free NSF/ANSI 372 requirement for any pipe carrying water for human consumption.

Common Questions

Do I have to repipe my whole house at once?

Not necessarily. A partial repipe addressing only the worst-affected sections is an option, though a whole-home repipe avoids repeating the disruption again a few years later.

How long does a whole-home repipe take?

Most residential repipes take two to five days depending on the home's size and how accessible the existing walls and crawlspace are.

Will repiping increase my home's value?

New plumbing is generally viewed favorably by buyers and inspectors, particularly in a home where the alternative is visible original galvanized pipe from the early 20th century.

Do I need to move out during a repipe?

Most homeowners stay in the home during a repipe, though water gets shut off in sections at a time as work progresses through the house.

How much wall access does a repipe actually require?

Some drywall access is typically needed to route new lines, which gets patched as part of the job, though the exact amount varies based on the home's layout and existing access points.

Can a repipe be done in phases if I cannot afford it all at once?

Yes, though phasing it means the disruption happens more than once. If budget allows, completing it in one project avoids repeating the wall access and patching process later.

The Bottom Line

The decision to repipe usually comes down to pattern versus incident. One leak is an incident worth a repair. A pattern of leaks, discoloration, or pressure loss across the house is worth a serious repipe conversation before the next failure happens somewhere less convenient, like behind a finished wall or under a slab. Getting an honest assessment of which situation you are actually in, rather than assuming the worst or hoping a run of bad luck will end on its own, is the first step either way.
