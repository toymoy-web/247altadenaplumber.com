What to Do When Your Toilet Won't Stop Running

By the Altadena 24/7 Plumbing team

Call Now

A toilet that keeps running after you flush, or runs on its own periodically, is almost always a flapper or a fill valve problem. Both are inexpensive fixes once you know which one it is.

The Flapper Is The First Thing To Check

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush and drops back down to seal the tank. Over time it wears, warps, or collects mineral buildup that keeps it from sealing fully.

You can check this yourself: remove the tank lid and watch the flapper after a flush. If it does not sit flat and sealed once the tank refills, that is your problem, and a replacement flapper is an inexpensive hardware store fix.

If The Flapper Looks Fine, Check The Fill Valve

The fill valve controls how much water enters the tank after a flush. If it does not shut off at the right water level, water continuously trickles into the overflow tube, which sounds like running water even though the flapper is sealed properly.

Adjusting the fill valve's float level sometimes resolves a minor case, but a valve that has failed internally usually needs replacement rather than adjustment.

Why This Matters More Than It Seems

A running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons a day, which shows up as a real, avoidable increase on your water bill. It is one of the most common causes we find when someone calls about an unexplained bill spike.

When To Consider Replacing The Toilet Instead

If the toilet predates California's current 1.28-gallon low-flow standard, which took effect for new installations years ago, repeated part-by-part repairs on an old, inefficient unit often make less sense than replacing it with a modern low-flow model.

A toilet with a hairline crack in the bowl or tank is also a replacement situation regardless of age, since a cracked bowl cannot be reliably repaired.

Why Altadena's Hard Water Makes This More Common

Toilets across Altadena's mutual water company service areas are exposed to real hardness variation, and mineral buildup on a flapper seal or inside a fill valve mechanism is a common reason a toilet starts running intermittently after functioning fine for years.

That mineral buildup does not happen overnight. It accumulates gradually, which is why a toilet that ran perfectly fine for a decade can suddenly develop a running problem with no clear single trigger.

When A Professional Repair Makes More Sense Than DIY

A rocking toilet, one that shifts slightly when you sit on it, needs its flange inspected before any new wax ring goes in, since a loose base will fail a fresh seal the same way it failed the old one. This is a case where a quick flapper swap will not solve the underlying problem.

Water at the base of the toilet, rather than inside the tank, usually means the wax ring seal has failed, which requires pulling the toilet to replace, a bigger job than most homeowners want to tackle without experience.

Common Questions

How much water does a running toilet actually waste?

A significant, continuously running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons a day. Even a minor, quiet leak past the flapper adds up meaningfully over a billing cycle.

Can I replace a flapper myself?

Yes, this is one of the more DIY-friendly plumbing repairs. Bring your old flapper to the hardware store to match the size, or check your toilet model's specifications.

Why does my toilet randomly run for a few seconds every so often?

This is called a phantom flush, and it usually means the flapper is letting a small amount of water leak into the bowl slowly enough that the tank has to periodically top itself off.

Is it normal for a toilet to run right after installation?

No. A newly installed toilet should not run continuously. If it does, the fill valve was likely not adjusted correctly during installation and needs a follow-up check.

Can hard water cause a toilet to run more often?

Yes. Mineral buildup on the flapper seal or inside the fill valve mechanism is a common reason a toilet starts running intermittently after functioning fine for years.

Should both toilets in my house be replaced at the same time if one is old?

Not necessarily. If only one is showing problems, replacing that one is reasonable, though matching fixtures can be a consideration for a full bathroom remodel.

The Bottom Line

A running toilet is one of the most fixable plumbing annoyances there is, and it is almost never worth ignoring given how much it can add to a water bill over just a few months. Check the flapper first, then the fill valve, before assuming you need a new toilet, and call for help if the base itself feels loose or unstable. A five minute check now can save months of quietly wasted water and a bill that keeps creeping up without an obvious explanation.
