Tankless vs. Tank Water Heater: Which Is Right for Your Home?

By the Altadena 24/7 Plumbing team

Replacing a water heater is a good moment to reconsider whether a traditional tank or a tankless unit fits your household better, since the two solve hot water differently rather than one simply being an upgrade of the other.

How Each One Actually Works

A tank water heater stores and continuously reheats a set volume of water, typically 40 to 50 gallons for a household. Once that volume runs out, you wait for it to reheat.

A tankless unit heats water on demand as it passes through a heat exchanger, with no storage tank at all. In theory, you never run out of hot water, though output is limited by the unit's flow rate rather than a stored volume.

Upfront Cost And Installation

Tank units cost less upfront and install more simply in most homes, since they typically fit into a home's existing venting and connections.

Tankless units cost more upfront, and retrofitting one into a home built for a tank sometimes requires new venting or gas line upsizing, which adds to the installation cost beyond the unit itself.

Ongoing Costs And Lifespan

Tankless units generally last longer than tank units, often reaching 20 years compared to a tank's typical 8 to 12, and they use less energy since there is no standby heat loss from a stored volume.

In a hard water area, a tankless unit's heat exchanger is more sensitive to mineral scale than a tank's larger interior, which is why manufacturers recommend annual descaling in areas with Altadena's water hardness.

Which One Fits Your Household

A smaller household with modest hot water demand often does fine with a properly sized tank unit at a lower upfront cost.

A larger household, especially one where multiple bathrooms run hot water at the same time, benefits more from tankless output, or from a higher-capacity tank sized specifically for that peak demand.

A home going through a rebuild or full remodel is a natural point to reconsider this decision, since the gas line and venting are often being planned fresh anyway.

What Changes If You Are Comparing Options For A Rebuild

A home rebuilding after the Eaton Fire is planning gas lines and venting fresh rather than working around existing infrastructure, which removes one of the biggest cost differences between the two options, since retrofitting venting for a tankless switch is no longer a factor.

That makes the tank-versus-tankless decision more about long-term household demand and available gas line capacity for the whole home's appliance list, rather than which option is cheaper to simply drop into an existing setup.

Sizing Either Option Correctly

Sizing starts with household size and peak hot water demand, comparing a standard tank's stored volume against a tankless unit's on-demand output for your home's actual usage pattern, not a generic household average.

For a gas tankless unit specifically, confirming the existing gas line can support the new unit's BTU demand is a necessary step before installation, since an undersized line is one of the more common reasons an installation gets delayed at inspection.

Common Questions

Does a tankless unit really provide unlimited hot water?

It provides continuous hot water as long as demand stays within the unit's rated flow capacity. Running multiple showers and a dishwasher simultaneously can still exceed a single unit's output.

Is it worth switching from tank to tankless during a remodel?

A remodel is one of the best times to make the switch, since venting and gas line changes are often already part of the project scope.

How much maintenance does a tankless unit need compared to a tank?

A tankless unit needs annual descaling in a hard water area, while a tank unit benefits from periodic flushing, though it is somewhat more forgiving if that maintenance is skipped occasionally.

Can I install a tankless unit in the exact same spot as my old tank?

Sometimes, but tankless units often need different venting than a tank, so the exact same location is not always possible without some additional work.

Which option is better for a rebuild or new construction?

Either works well for new construction, and the choice usually comes down to available gas line capacity, available space, and household size rather than one being universally better.

Does a tankless unit work during a power outage?

A gas tankless unit typically needs electricity for its ignition system, so most will not operate during a power outage even though the fuel source itself is gas rather than electric.

The Bottom Line

There is no universally right answer between tank and tankless. The right choice depends on your household's actual hot water demand, your budget for the upfront cost, and whether you are already planning other changes to your home's gas and venting setup that would make a tankless switch easier to justify right now rather than later. Whichever direction you lean, sizing the unit correctly for your actual usage matters more than the general reputation of either option.
